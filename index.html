Civics: meaning and importance

What civics is, where it comes from, why it matters and its key concepts.

What is civics?

Civics is the study of the rights, duties and responsibilities of citizenship.

Definition

Civics is the study of the rights, duties and responsibilities of citizenship. It includes understanding how government operates, how laws are created and how citizens can actively participate in the democratic process.

Civics education

Civics education helps individuals become informed, responsible citizens who can contribute to their communities and to society at large.

What civics teaches

  • What your rights are.
  • What your duties and responsibilities are.
  • How the government and laws work.
  • How you can take part in public life.

Origin and development of civics

Civics began with small city-states and direct democracy; today it covers national and global concerns.

Humans are social beings

People flourish in communities. The classical idea (associated with the Greek philosopher Aristotle) is that individuals reach their full potential through cooperation and exchange within a society.

Ancient Greek and Roman city-states

  • The city-state, or “polis”, was the centre of civilised life.
  • Each city-state was an independent political entity with its own culture, social traditions and government.
  • Transport and communication were limited, so contact between city-states was minimal.
  • So the study of civics was limited to the immediate concerns of life within the city-state.

Direct democracy in the Greek period

Citizens took part directly in decision-making instead of electing representatives to do it for them.

The modern period

  • Societies became larger and more complex; citizens now live in larger political units like countries made up of many cities, towns and villages.
  • A citizen's relationships and responsibilities have grown beyond the local community to include national and even global concerns.
  • Civics now helps citizens understand this complex world by promoting values, norms and understanding of current social and political challenges.

Significance of civics (7 reasons)

Civics shapes informed, responsible individuals who contribute to their community and country.
1
Flow of Information

Civics educates citizens about their fundamental rights and duties, so they understand how society works. By spreading information about laws, rights and responsibilities it keeps citizens informed and engaged.

2
Understanding Rights and Responsibilities

Modern society is built on a balance between what citizens are entitled to (rights) and what they are expected to do (responsibilities). Civics promotes a culture of accountability and respect for the law.

3
Political Participation

Civics encourages active participation in political life: voting in elections, understanding government policies and staying informed about current issues. In a democracy, citizens are the foundation of political authority.

4
Global Awareness

Civics expands understanding beyond one's own country, makes people aware of global issues and fosters international cooperation. It helps people think about how their actions affect others around the globe.

5
Comprehension of Economic Issues

Economic issues shape societies. Civics educates people about their economic rights and responsibilities and the broader economic principles that affect their communities. It helps people adopt balanced economic values and avoid unethical practices.

6
Character Building

A main goal of civics is to build strong character through values such as honesty, fairness, tolerance and integrity. These civic virtues help create a society of responsible, ethical citizens.

7
Community Welfare

Civics stresses community welfare and encourages citizens to work together on problems like poverty, unemployment and healthcare. Governments cannot address every issue alone; citizens must take responsibility for their communities' well-being through volunteerism and social activism.

Key concepts in civics

Five core ideas: citizenship, democracy, rule of law, public participation, civic virtue.
1
Citizenship

The rights, responsibilities and duties of individuals within a society. It includes participation in governance, obeying laws and contributing to the welfare of the community.

2
Democracy

A form of government in which power is vested in the people, either directly or through elected representatives. It stresses political participation, rule of law and protection of individual rights.

3
Rule of Law

The principle that all members of society, including government officials, are subject to the law. It ensures fairness, equality and justice.

4
Public Participation

The process by which individuals take part in decision-making in society. It includes voting, policy discussions and community activism.

5
Civic Virtue

Developing the habits essential for the success of a democratic society, including honesty, responsibility and respect for others.

Citizenship

What citizenship means, who is a citizen, its types, rights, responsibilities and principles.

Conceptual understanding of citizenship

Citizenship = formal membership of a nation, with rights in return for obligations.

Membership and rights

Citizenship is the formal acknowledgment of an individual's membership in a nation. It gives specific rights, including the ability to vote and the assurance of legal protection.

Obligations

Alongside the rights, citizenship involves obligations such as obeying laws, paying taxes and, in some cases, military service.

A two-way relationship

It is a dynamic relationship between the individual and the state, marked by mutual loyalty in exchange for rights and protection.

Active engagement

It also means active engagement in the community: taking part in political, social and economic life, fulfilling civic responsibilities and contributing to society through public service and community involvement.

Definitions of citizenship

Four scholars: Marshall, Miliband, Miller, Kymlicka. Learn one key idea each.
T. H. Marshall

“Citizenship is a status bestowed on those who are full members of a community. All who possess the status are equal with respect to the rights and duties with which the status is endowed.”

Key idea: status, equality of rights and duties.

Ralph Miliband

“Citizenship is the bond that unites people with the state, giving them both rights and duties, and making them active participants in the civic life of the political community.”

Key idea: a bond with the state; active participants.

David Miller

“Citizenship is the set of rights and obligations that gives individuals membership in a political community, offering them protection by the state and requiring them to contribute to its wellbeing.”

Key idea: rights and obligations; protection and contribution.

Will Kymlicka

“Citizenship is a relationship between an individual and a political community in which the individual participates actively in democratic governance while enjoying protection of their rights and freedoms.”

Key idea: relationship; active participation in democratic governance.

Classification of citizens: natural-born and naturalized

A citizen is either natural-born (citizen automatically at birth) or naturalized (becomes a citizen later through a legal process).
1. Natural-born citizen

Acquires citizenship automatically at birth: by being born on the country's territory, or through parentage (inherited from one or both citizen parents), whatever the place of birth.

  • Citizenship is automatic at birth.
  • No legal or government process is needed to acquire it.
  • Rights and responsibilities are usually granted from birth.
  • In many countries only natural-born citizens can hold the highest political offices, such as the presidency in the U.S.
2. Naturalized citizen

Acquires citizenship after birth through a legal process called naturalization.

  • Granted after completing the legal naturalization process.
  • May need to meet residency requirements (living in the country for some years), pass language tests and show knowledge of the country's history and laws.
  • Often involves showing good moral character and taking an oath of allegiance.
  • Usually has the same rights and responsibilities as natural-born citizens, though in some countries there are limits on certain offices (for example the U.S. presidency).

Types of citizenship (9 types)

Citizenship is more than a passport or following laws: today it has nine types, from active to inclusive.
1
Active citizenship

Practical participation in societal and community efforts; going beyond legal duties to help society through civic engagement, volunteering and advocacy.

  • Taking initiative in community projects.
  • Advocating for social justice or environmental causes.
  • Engaging in public service and civic discussions.
2
Digital citizenship

The role individuals play in the online world: using technology responsibly, joining online discussions and advocating for issues through digital platforms.

  • Ethical online behaviour, such as avoiding cyberbullying.
  • Online activism, from petitions to social media campaigns.
  • Staying informed on global and local issues through digital resources.
3
Global citizenship

A sense of responsibility toward the global community; seeing oneself as part of a larger world and acting on international challenges like climate change, human rights and global inequality.

  • Advocating for global issues such as poverty reduction and environmental sustainability.
  • Taking part in international collaborations and global advocacy.
  • Promoting peace and cultural understanding across borders.
4
Ecological citizenship

Focuses on the individual's role in protecting and sustaining the natural environment through sustainable practices and conservation.

  • Eco-friendly practices such as recycling and reducing carbon emissions.
  • Supporting policies and movements that protect natural resources.
  • Taking part in environmental activism and conservation efforts.
5
Corporate citizenship

The ethical responsibilities of businesses as members of society. Corporations are expected to address social, economic and environmental issues and contribute positively to their communities.

  • Implementing corporate social responsibility (CSR) initiatives.
  • Ensuring ethical business practices and transparency.
  • Committing to sustainability and philanthropy.
6
Passive citizenship

Limited or minimal engagement in civic activities. These individuals fulfil basic legal duties such as paying taxes but do not actively take part in civic or political matters.

  • Minimal or sporadic voting.
  • Limited involvement in community or public affairs.
  • Focus on personal or private concerns rather than broader societal issues.
7
Economic citizenship

Participation in the economic system as employees, consumers and entrepreneurs; emphasises financial accountability and awareness of economic policies and their societal impact.

  • Contributing to the economy through employment or entrepreneurship.
  • Meeting financial obligations like paying taxes.
  • Advocating for fair economic policies and labour rights.
8
Informed citizenship

Being well educated on contemporary issues, government policies and societal challenges, so decisions are thoughtful and based on facts.

  • Staying informed on political and social developments.
  • Engaging with credible information sources.
  • Taking part in thoughtful discussions and debates.
9
Inclusive citizenship

Ensures everyone, regardless of background or identity, has equal rights and opportunities to take part in society; promotes equity, social justice and non-discrimination.

  • Advocating for marginalized and underrepresented groups.
  • Promoting policies that encourage equality and inclusion.
  • Supporting accessibility and non-discriminatory practices in all areas of public life.

Rights of citizenship

A right is something the citizen receives: five key rights.
1
Freedom of expression

The right to express opinions without government censorship or retaliation.

2
Right to vote

The right to take part in democratic elections and choose representatives.

3
Right to a fair trial

Protection against arbitrary detention and access to due process under the law.

4
Freedom of assembly

The right to gather peacefully for protests, discussions or social movements.

5
Right to education

Access to education as a fundamental human right, to ensure equal opportunities.

Responsibilities of citizenship

A responsibility is something the citizen must do: five key responsibilities.
1
Obeying laws

Following the legal framework ensures order and security in society.

2
Paying taxes

Funding public services and infrastructure through taxation.

3
Serving on a jury

Taking part in the judicial system when called upon.

4
Participating in the democratic process

Staying informed, voting in elections and joining civic discussions.

5
Defending the country

Contributing to national defence when required, including military service or civil support.

Five principles: equality, rule of law, participation, responsibility, liberty.
1
Equality

Ensures all citizens are treated fairly and have equal opportunities.

2
Rule of law

No one is above the law, and everyone is subject to the same legal standards.

3
Participation

Encourages citizens to take part in governance through voting, public discussion and community initiatives.

4
Responsibility

Highlights the duty of individuals to contribute positively to society.

5
Liberty

Protects the freedom of individuals to live as they choose, within the limits of the law.

Civic and community engagement

What engagement means, who defined it, why it matters, how to take part and what gets in the way.

What is civic engagement?

Civic engagement = “the interactions of citizens with their society and their government”.

Meaning

Civic engagement is the involvement of individuals in activities that address issues of public concern. It includes voting, volunteering, attending public meetings and advocating for policy changes. It fosters a sense of community responsibility and makes sure individuals play an active role in shaping society.

A term with no single definition

In social and political science the term has no single definition. It is also used interchangeably with civic participation. It can be described as regular citizens' participation in community life to improve living conditions for others.

Purpose and examples

Kinds of civic engagement differ in context, scope and aim. The most common purpose is to contribute to a community's social, political or cultural life to create meaningful change, for example through volunteering in education or environmental protection, taking part in election campaigns, or working on advocacy projects and charity events.

Definitions of civic engagement

Three scholars: Delli Carpini, Ehrlich, Van Benshoten.
Michael Delli Carpini

“Civic engagement includes both paid and unpaid forms of political activism, environmentalism and national service. It can include efforts to directly address an issue, work with others in a community to solve a problem, or interact with the institutions of representative democracy.”

Key idea: paid and unpaid activism; solve problems; interact with democratic institutions.

Thomas Ehrlich

“Civic engagement means working to make a difference in the civic life of our communities and developing the combination of knowledge, skills, values and motivation to make that difference. It means promoting the quality of life in a community, through both political and non-political processes.”

Key idea: make a difference; knowledge + skills + values + motivation; political and non-political.

Van Benshoten

“Civic engagement may be defined as the means by which an individual, through collective action, influences the larger civil society.”

Key idea: an individual, through collective action, influences civil society.

Community engagement and related terms

Community engagement, public participation, volunteerism and advocacy are different but connected ways of taking part.
1
Community engagement

Collaboration between individuals, organisations and institutions to improve the quality of life in a community. It stresses building partnerships and engaging citizens in meaningful, productive ways to address local and global challenges.

2
Public participation

The involvement of individuals and groups in the decision-making processes of government and other public institutions: attending public meetings, giving feedback on policies and joining public consultations.

3
Volunteerism

Offering time and services to others without financial gain. A key part of civic engagement, such as mentoring youth or joining community clean-up drives.

4
Advocacy

Supporting and arguing for a cause, policy or proposal: writing to elected officials, taking part in protests and using social media to raise awareness.

Importance of civic engagement (7 reasons)

Civic engagement builds a strong, vibrant and cohesive society: it helps people contribute to the common good, build stronger communities and promote democracy and social justice.
1
Strengthening the communities

Brings people together to address local problems, improve public spaces and help those in need, through volunteerism or community activism. Engaged citizens create stronger, more resilient communities.

2
Promotion of democratic participation

Active involvement ensures democracy works effectively. Voting, advocacy and public forums give citizens a voice, hold elected officials accountable and make government actions reflect the will of the people.

3
Empowerment of citizens

Gives individuals the chance to contribute to positive change, develop leadership skills and connect with like-minded people. It also builds communication, problem-solving and organisational abilities.

4
Supporting social justice

Addresses inequalities and advocates for marginalized communities. Through activism, service and political participation, citizens can challenge systemic injustices and promote fairness, equality and inclusion.

5
Enhancing civic responsibility

Encourages citizens to take responsibility for the welfare of their communities. By working to improve local conditions, individuals develop a sense of shared responsibility and commitment to others' well-being, reinforcing the social fabric.

6
Construction of social networks

Builds strong networks and relationships among community members. These networks are the base for collaboration, support during crises and collective problem-solving, improving overall quality of life.

7
Government accountability

Citizens monitor government actions and policies so officials remain accountable. Through discussion, advocacy and activism they can push for transparency and reform, reducing corruption and inefficiency.

Ways to engage in civic and community life

Five ways: volunteering, advocacy, public meetings, community projects, political involvement.
1
Volunteering

Giving time and effort to community projects, charities and social causes.

2
Advocacy

Supporting and promoting policies and causes through public campaigns, petitions and lobbying.

3
Attending public meetings

Taking part in discussions on local governance and policy-making.

4
Community projects

Joining initiatives such as environmental clean-ups, food drives and mentorship programmes.

5
Political involvement

Running for office, supporting candidates or joining political parties.

Challenges to civic engagement

Four obstacles: lack of awareness, political apathy, misinformation, barriers to participation.
1
Lack of awareness

Many people do not know their civic rights and responsibilities, which leads to disengagement.

2
Political apathy

Some citizens feel disconnected from political processes and believe their participation makes no difference.

3
Misinformation

False or misleading information can influence public opinion and hinder effective civic engagement.

4
Barriers to participation

Economic, social or institutional obstacles can stop some groups from engaging fully in civic activities.

Conclusion (Lecture 1)

Civic and community engagement is essential for a just and democratic society. By understanding civic responsibilities and taking part in public life, individuals build stronger, more inclusive communities. Encouraging awareness, removing barriers to participation and building a culture of engagement ensures a better future for all.

Democracy

The concept of democracy, definitions, types and its relationship with citizenship.

Understanding the concept of democracy

Democracy is a political system based on the participation and consent of the people.

Where authority comes from

The authority of the government comes from the will of the citizens, who use their power either directly or through elected representatives.

Core principles

At its core democracy upholds equality, freedom and justice, so that every individual has an equal voice in decisions that affect their lives.

What citizens can do

  • Vote, express their opinions freely and take part in public affairs without fear of repression.
  • Choose and hold leaders accountable through regular, free and fair elections.

Representation and rights

  • Representation in government institutions makes sure diverse voices and interests are reflected in policy-making.
  • Democracy protects fundamental rights, including freedom of speech, religion and association.

Aim

A just political environment where power is exercised transparently and responsibly, in service of the public good.

Definitions of democracy

Four thinkers: Dahl, Schumpeter, Dewey, Lincoln.
Robert Dahl

“Democracy is a system of governance that responds fully to all its citizens as political equals, allowing them full opportunity to participate in the life of the community and influence collective decisions.”

Key idea: citizens as political equals; full opportunity to participate.

Joseph Schumpeter

“Democracy is that institutional arrangement for arriving at political decisions in which individuals acquire the power to decide by means of a competitive struggle for the people's vote.”

Key idea: competition for the people's vote.

John Dewey

“Democracy is more than a form of government; it is primarily a mode of associated living, of conjoint communicated experience.”

Key idea: a way of living together, not only a government.

Abraham Lincoln

“Democracy is the government of the people, by the people, for the people.”

Key idea: of, by and for the people (the most famous short definition).

Types of democracy (5 types)

Five types: direct, representative, constitutional, liberal, participatory.
1
Direct democracy

Citizens take part directly in decision-making, typically through referendums or assemblies. Example: ancient Athens, where eligible citizens voted directly on laws and policies.

2
Representative democracy

Citizens elect representatives who make decisions on their behalf. More common in modern, larger societies where direct participation in every decision is impractical. Examples: the United States, Pakistan and India.

3
Constitutional democracy

A form of representative democracy where the power of elected officials is limited by a constitution. The constitution ensures laws and government actions follow fundamental principles, such as protecting human rights and limiting governmental power.

4
Liberal democracy

A variant of representative democracy that emphasises individual rights, rule of law and protection of freedoms. The power of the majority is balanced by safeguarding the rights of minorities.

5
Participatory democracy

Encourages broader participation in decision-making beyond elections, promoting civic engagement and community involvement in governance. It stresses grassroots movements and public consultations.

Relationship between democracy and citizenship (9 points)

Democracy and citizenship depend on each other: citizens make democracy work, and democracy protects citizens' rights.
1
Citizenship as the foundation for democracy

Citizenship is the status that gives individuals rights and obligations in a political community, especially the entitlement to take part in the democratic process. Citizens are the key participants: they vote, run for office, join discussions and take part in civic activities. Without citizenship, individuals would lose the formal ways of influencing political choices, laws and policies.

2
Rights and obligations

In a democracy citizenship gives vital rights: vote, freedom of expression, assembly and equal protection under the law. It also brings responsibilities: adhering to laws, fulfilling tax obligations, serving on juries and contributing to collective welfare. Active citizenship is crucial for democracy to work.

3
Enhancement of community welfare

Democratic citizenship means being actively involved and well informed on political matters while using rights responsibly. This engagement shapes public policy and improves community welfare.

4
Participation and representation

Citizens should engage in governing their nation directly or through representatives. In a representative democracy they elect officials who advocate for their interests. Every individual has the right to take part on equal footing, which secures representation for everyone and prevents the marginalization of groups.

5
Citizenship as a mechanism for accountability

Citizens can challenge government actions through elections, protests and advocacy, especially when their interests are overlooked or officials are corrupt or misuse authority. This keeps governments tuned to the needs and aspirations of the people.

6
Education and awareness

Civic education fortifies democracy: a thriving democracy needs citizens who know their rights and duties. It equips people to make educated decisions in elections and join public discussions constructively. Active citizenship keeps people aware of social, political and economic matters.

7
Democracy enriches civic identity

The link between individuals and the state is built on equality, freedom and justice. This makes citizens proactive participants in the nation's social and political life and nurtures values such as tolerance, respect for others and dedication to the common good.

8
Empowerment and inclusion

Citizenship gives a voice in the democratic process, especially to marginalized groups. Inclusive citizenship lets all citizens, whatever their background, help shape policies. Feeling included fosters social cohesion and a sense of belonging.

9
Mutual reinforcement

Democracy and citizenship strengthen each other: a vibrant democracy encourages active citizenship, and engaged citizens sustain democratic institutions. The health of a democracy is often measured by the level of citizen involvement in governance.

Both are interdependent for their survival and sustainability.

Conclusion

The relationship between democracy and citizenship is vital for the health and sustainability of both. Citizenship provides the foundation for participation, while democracy creates an environment that respects and promotes citizens' rights and responsibilities. Understanding and nurturing this relationship helps societies move toward more inclusive, participatory and vibrant democracies that empower all citizens to contribute to the common good.

Modern society and citizenship

The 14 characteristics that form the foundation of modern society, and how they shape citizenship.

What is modern society?

Modern society = the characteristics, structures and dynamics that have developed since the industrial revolution and keep evolving.

The concept of modern society refers to the characteristics, structures and dynamics that have developed since the industrial revolution and continue to evolve in contemporary times. It reflects the social values and practices that shape people's lives today.

Foundations 1–7

Rule of law · human rights · industrialization · urbanization · secularization · bureaucracy · democratic governance.
1
Rule of law

Modern societies rest on a legal framework that ensures justice, security and order. All citizens, regardless of status, are equally subject to the law. Citizens have rights (freedom of expression, voting) and responsibilities (obeying laws, paying taxes).

2
Human rights and freedoms

Universal human rights are upheld: freedom of speech, equality under the law and protection from discrimination. Civil liberties such as freedom of religion, expression and privacy are central.

3
Industrialization

A shift from agrarian to industrial and service-based economies. Automation and mechanization raised productivity and changed how goods are produced, and enabled the flow of goods, services and information across borders.

4
Urbanization

Movement of populations from rural areas to cities. It led to large cities and metropolitan areas (centres of economic activity, social interaction and cultural exchange) and the growth of infrastructure like transport, healthcare, education and housing.

5
Secularization and the decline of religious authority

A shift away from religious authority. Secular institutions, including governments, legal systems and education, dominate public life and decide on rational and scientific principles rather than religious doctrine.

6
Bureaucracy

Max Weber's idea of rationalization: modern societies value efficiency, predictability and bureaucracy. Public and private institutions use organised, rule-based administration, stressing order, control and efficiency in decision-making.

7
Democratic governance and political participation

A trademark of modern society: citizens take part in political decision-making through free and fair elections. Modern democracies rest on individual rights, political freedom and the rule of law.

Foundations 8–14

Individualism · social mobility · multiculturalism · mass media · science and technology · social change · ethical dilemmas.
8
Individualism and personal autonomy

Personal autonomy and self-expression are highly valued. Individuals are encouraged to make choices about their lives, including careers, social roles and personal goals.

9
Social mobility

Greater potential for individuals to improve their socioeconomic status through merit, education and achievement, rather than being held by rigid social hierarchies.

10
Multiculturalism

Cultural pluralism: diverse ethnic, religious and linguistic groups coexist. It fosters inclusion and equality for all groups, enriches social interaction and has led to recognition of gender equality and other movements for civil rights and justice.

11
Mass media

Mass media and digital technology have transformed communication. Global connectivity lets information flow rapidly across borders, and social media platforms, the internet and digital communication reshape cultural dynamics and the spread of information.

12
Scientific knowledge and technological advancements

Modern societies are rooted in scientific knowledge and rational thinking; evidence and scientific principles inform decisions in healthcare, education and industry. Technology has transformed daily life but poses ethical dilemmas, such as the impact of AI and automation on jobs.

13
Social change

Modern societies are dynamic. Technology, economic development and shifts in political ideology drive rapid change, alongside challenges such as environmental sustainability, economic inequality and climate change.

14
Ethical dilemmas in modern society

Scientific and technological progress raises moral questions: data privacy, artificial intelligence, environmental degradation and genetic engineering. Balancing progress with ethics is a key challenge.

How modern society shapes citizenship (conclusion)

Modern society gives citizens rights and freedoms and expects duties in return; citizenship is also active participation.

Rights and obligations

The relationship between modern society and citizenship is rooted in rights and responsibilities. With its foundations in the rule of law, democracy, human rights and individualism, modern society ensures legal protection and grants freedoms such as voting and expression. In return, citizens are expected to obey laws, pay taxes and take part in civic duties.

Participation

Modern societies encourage citizens to take an active part in political processes, influence decisions and hold officials accountable.

Inclusion and global links

Modern society promotes inclusion, diversity and social mobility, enriching citizenship. Through technology, mass media and global connectivity, citizens are more informed and engaged, and can address global challenges like environmental sustainability and economic inequality.

Citizenship in modern society is not only legal status but also active participation in shaping the social, political and economic landscape, locally and globally.
